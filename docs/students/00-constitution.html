Part 1: The Constitution

Our aspiration and our sources

We seek to establish a world-class culture. Following Brigham Young’s admonition to gather up all the good and true principles wherever we find them, we draw inspiration from many places, with particular emphasis on four organizations:

  • Amazon, Leadership Principles
  • Bridgewater, Original Philosophy
  • McKinsey, Purpose, Mission, and Values
  • The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, Christlike Attributes, Preach My Gospel, Chapter 6

What follows is our synthesis: the principles we commit to live by.

Our principles

  1. Excellence over everything. If forced to choose between excellence and money, we choose excellence. We hold standards outsiders might consider unreasonably high, on purpose. Every deliverable carries our name and BYU’s.
  2. Radical truthfulness. Accuracy in understanding reality is the foundation everything else rests on. Say the uncomfortable thing in the room, not the hallway.
  3. Ideas win on merit, not rank. Anyone may respectfully challenge anyone, including the faculty advisor. Once a decision is made, disagree and commit.
  4. Candor with charity. Truth without love is brutality. Assume good intent, listen before answering, and care about the person while being honest about the work.
  5. Act in faith. Faith is a principle of action, not a feeling of certainty. Move forward on incomplete information; bias for action.
  6. Mistakes are raw material. A mistake surfaced quickly and studied honestly makes the whole team smarter; a mistake hidden makes the whole team weaker. Pain plus reflection equals progress.
  7. Client interests first. Put the client ahead of ourselves, preserve confidences, never oversell what we or our tools can do, and deliver improvement that lasts beyond the engagement.
  8. Own your outcomes. Take responsibility without excuse. No one here says “that’s not my job.”
  9. Everyone teaches, everyone learns. We are an apprenticeship. Everyone should leave the Foundry more capable than they arrived.
  10. The red face test. When a situation is ambiguous, ask: could I explain this decision to a respected leader, say Clark Gilbert, Shane Reese, or Brigitte Madrian, without being red in the face? If not, do not do it.

Part 2: The Operating Model

Flat hierarchy

Client teams operate without hierarchy. A team may be composed of 1 undergrad and 1 MBA, 2 undergrads, or 2 MBAs; in some cases teams may be slightly larger. In all cases, teams are flat with no hierarchy. Every member of the team has equal accountability and equal authority. Degree program, seniority, or AI Foundry leadership role confer no rank inside a team.

What this means in practice:

  • Self-organization. Teams align on work streams themselves. They divide work by strength and interest, not by status.
  • Equal accountability. The team owns the outcome together. If a deliverable slips or misses the bar, that belongs to everyone on the team.
  • Peer feedback. Team members give feedback directly to each other as the first and normal channel.
  • Escalation. When an issue cannot be resolved within the team, escalations go through JD and Scott.
  • Resetting teams. If a team does not work well together it can be recomposed. This is a normal operating mechanism, not a punishment.

Two courses, two roles

The AI Foundry is a cross-listed class with an undergraduate section (STRAT 490R) and a graduate section (MBA 693R). For cross-listed courses, earning graduate credit is required to carry requirements beyond the undergraduate expectations. In the AI Foundry, that requirement is met by adding an organization leadership role: students in the graduate section help run the AI Foundry itself.

  • Students in the undergraduate section build. Their job is excellent client work, similar to Sandbox, except building for clients instead of for yourself.
  • Students in the graduate section build plus administer the organization itself. They do the same client work, and in addition take on a leadership role in running the AI Foundry as an organization: operations, client pipeline, quality, recruiting, and culture.
Note

With permission from the faculty advisor, undergraduates are eligible to enroll in the graduate section and be considered for AI Foundry leadership roles.

The two sections with associated roles for those enrolled in the graduate section does not violate the flat hierarchy principle. Inside a client team, an MBA, or an undergraduate enrolled in the graduate section, has no rank over a student enrolled in the undergraduate section. Every team member has equal accountability and authority for the client work they are staffed to. The leadership roles are a different responsibility expected only in the graduate course and do not invoke any kind of authority over a teammate.

The Ask First feedback culture

Everyone is empowered to give feedback to anyone at any time, respectfully and directly. More important, our norm is to regularly ask for feedback. Asking removes the awkwardness of unsolicited criticism, signals humility, and puts each of us in control of our own growth.

A practical habit: at the end of a project, a meeting, or a deliverable review, ask at least one person “what is one thing I could have done better?” Feedback flows in every direction, including to the faculty advisor. In general, teams should carve out 15 minutes for mutual feedback once every two weeks.
