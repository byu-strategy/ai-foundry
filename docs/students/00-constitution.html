Part 1: The AI Foundry Constitution

We seek to establish a world-class culture. Following Brigham Young’s admonition to gather up all the good and true principles wherever we find them, we draw inspiration from many places, including:

  • BYU Marriott School of Business, Vision, Mission, and Values
  • The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, the standard works
  • The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, Christlike Attributes, Preach My Gospel, Chapter 6
  • Amazon, Leadership Principles
  • Bridgewater, Original Philosophy
  • McKinsey, Purpose, Mission, and Values
  • John Wooden, The Pyramid of Success
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II

Our principles

Lists of values often list principles to be maximized such as: be excellent, be truthful, be bold. Such lists are helpful but often suffer from two limitations.

  1. Almost any good quality can be taken to excess. Excellence can overshoot into perfectionism. Boldness can overshoot into recklessness. A simple list of values usually provides no way to acknowledge “it’s possible to do too much of a good thing.”

  2. Two good principles may collide with each other. Speed collides with rigor. Candor collides with kindness. Excellence collides with a deadline.

Fortunately, Aristotle elegantly solved both limitations by using a concept he called the Golden Mean. The character traits we seek often sit between two failure modes: doing too little or doing too much. Courage is not the opposite of cowardice; it sits as a Golden Mean, that is, a weighted average, between cowardice and recklessness. Generosity sits between stinginess and waste.

Aristotle was clear that the Golden Mean is not fixed by a formula but rather found by good judgment, and good judgment comes from reflecting on real experience, either your own or that of others.

Aristotle called these qualities virtues. We call them principles, which is more modern language for the same concept. Each of the fourteen below is written with possible failure modes named.

These principles are aspirational and we may frequently miss the Golden Mean. Yet we keep striving, and over time develop true wisdom and good judgment.

We are successful when we can honestly say this of ourselves:

“Success is peace of mind which is a direct result of self-satisfaction in knowing you made the effort to become the best of which you are capable.”

John Wooden

When there is no golden mean

Aristotle is explicit that the idea of the golden mean has limits: some things “imply by their names that they are themselves bad,” and of those “it is not possible, then, ever to be right with regard to them.”

Sometimes principles collide

They will. Rigor says check it again; the deadline says ship. Courage says say it now; discernment says not in this room, not in front of these people. Excellence says raise the bar; patience says this is not the week.

Three things can be helpful when collisions occur:

Name the collision out loud. “I think rigor and the deadline are fighting here” is a faster route to a decision than arguing the merits as though only one principle were in play.

Correct against your own pull. Aristotle’s coaching instruction is that aiming straight at the golden mean undershoots, because your own habit is already bending you. Learn which way you personally miss, then overcorrect. “We shall get into the intermediate state by drawing well away from error, as people do in straightening sticks that are bent.”

The red face test. When a situation is genuinely ambiguous, ask: could I explain this decision to a respected leader without being red in the face? If not, do not do it.

Part 2: AI Foundry Operating Model

Three pillars

The AI Foundry is a consultancy and a product studio, with a research lab planned for the future.

  • Consultancy. Client projects for outside organizations. Client work keeps us grounded in real problems and current with what organizations need, and client fees fund the Foundry.
  • Product studio. Products the Foundry builds and runs for itself, such as the Learning Harness and the AI Foundry app. What we learn from clients shapes what we build.
  • Research lab (future). Research that grows out of what we build and see with clients.

Each faculty member owns a portfolio of clients end to end, from finding them to delivering the work, and advises the teams that serve them. The Foundry grows by adding faculty.

Client teams

A client team is two people: a Senior Builder-Consultant, who is a second-year MBA, and a Builder-Consultant, who is an undergraduate junior or senior. Both build and both consult. The Senior Builder-Consultant also owns the relationship with the client and directs the work: priorities, deliverables, dates, and review before the client sees it.

Every team carries two projects: a client project and a Foundry product. Client work comes first. When it slows, the Foundry product keeps the team building, so no one waits on a client.

Each team has a Project Advisor, a faculty member who meets with the team every week for sprint review and planning and holds the standard of the work. The Engagement Success Guide sets out what clients expect and how a team works.

What this means in practice:

  • Shared accountability. The team owns the outcome together. If a deliverable slips or misses the bar, that belongs to both of you.
  • Direct feedback. Teammates give feedback to each other first, in both directions.
  • Escalation. When an issue cannot be resolved within the team, bring it to your Project Advisor, and then to Professor Murff.
  • Resetting teams. If a team does not work well together it can be recomposed. This is a normal operating mechanism, not a punishment.

Two sections, two roles

The AI Foundry is cross-listed, with an undergraduate section (STRAT 490R) and a graduate section (MBA 693R). Graduate credit has to carry requirements beyond the undergraduate expectations. In the AI Foundry, that requirement is met by leading the client team.

  • Students in the undergraduate section are Builder-Consultants. Their job is excellent work for the client and the Foundry.
  • Students in the graduate section are Senior Builder-Consultants. They do the same work and, in addition, own the client relationship and direct the team’s work.

Foundry Operations

Foundry Operations is a paid team of three second-year MBAs, hired as BYU student employees, who run the Foundry as an organization alongside Professor Murff:

  • Chief of Staff: client operations, the Foundry’s records, and the team’s weekly priorities. Reports to Professor Murff.
  • Grants and Fundraising Lead: grants and donor support, with the Marriott School’s advancement office.
  • Marketing and Events Lead: the newsletter, the Foundry’s brand voice, recruiting, and events.

Foundry Operations audits the course and Strategy Leadership Lab and works together on Thursday mornings.

Students may also attend the Foundry’s Tuesday instruction without registering.

The Ask First feedback culture

Everyone is empowered to give feedback to anyone at any time, respectfully and directly. More important, our norm is to regularly ask for feedback. Asking removes the awkwardness of unsolicited criticism, signals humility, and puts each of us in control of our own growth. A practical habit: at the end of a project, a meeting, or a deliverable review, ask at least one person “what is one thing I could have done better?” Feedback flows in every direction, including to the faculty advisor. In general, teams should carve out 15 minutes for mutual feedback once every two weeks.

The most useful version of that question, given how Part 1 is written, is directional: which way did I miss? Too little or too much is a far easier thing for a teammate to answer than a rating, and it is the only kind of answer you can act on.
