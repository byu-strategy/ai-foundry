# The Developmentship Model

*Draft. Supersedes the Google Doc of the same name, 2026-08-12.*

Developmentship is our practice of developing one another through shared work, and it is how
your individual grade is determined.

Your grade is not the professor's opinion of you. It is a synthesis of structured input from
the people you actually worked with, gathered against four named things, every one of which is
something a person visibly does.

## 1. One instrument: the Consultant diamond

The model has one measuring instrument. Four axes, rated 1 to 5, observed by other people.

| Axis | Imperative behind it | Judged on |
|---|---|---|
| **Workstream Ownership** | Move Work Forward | Whether you drive the work |
| **Collaboration** | Move Work Forward | Whether people want you on their team |
| **Communication** | Create Impact with People | The artifacts: updates, decks, memos |
| **Client Presence** | Create Impact with People | The conduct: how you show up in front of someone senior |

Four ratings, not sixteen. A reviewer can hold four judgments and mean them. Ask for sixteen
and they will straight-line the form.

Two axes point inward at the work and the team. Two point outward at the client and the reader.
That is the whole diamond.

### Why not the Builder hexagon

Not because building does not matter. Because the hexagon is graded in the other class, from
different evidence.

The rule across both courses is that **demonstrated proficiency is graded and a self-rating
never is.** In the Build course the six Builder axes organize the graded work: the sprints, the
codebase architecture analysis, peer code review, peer product testing. A student's standing on
an axis there comes from an artifact somebody else evaluated.

The baseline survey is a third thing, and it is graded nowhere. It is a development instrument
you use on yourself and an input to how you get staffed. The moment a self-report carries grade
weight it stops measuring anything, and grading the August-to-December change is worse, because
it rewards understating the baseline. It would also cost you the staffing signal, which is what
the survey is actually load-bearing for.

| Instrument | Graded on | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Builder hexagon | The artifacts you produced | Build course |
| Consultant diamond | The conduct others observed | The Foundry Lab |
| Baseline self-assessment | Not graded, anywhere | Survey, September and December |

Same six axes in the first and third rows, read two different ways. Keeping them apart is what
makes each one honest.

### Why not the deliverable

The client work is graded already, through the milestone and client evaluation components of
the syllabus. Those are team-level and they measure the product. This model is individual and
it measures the person. Do not make either one carry the other's job.

## 2. Where the principles live

The constitution is the standard. The four axes are where it becomes visible.

Nobody is rated on a principle, because a principle is not a thing you can rate. It is a thing
you can point at. So the principles do not appear on the rating form at all. They are built into
the questions the professor asks, and they are the language of the development summary that
comes back to you.

A student living the principles rates high on this diamond. A student rating low is failing a
principle that can be named. That coupling is the whole design, and it is why the model is
worth running.

## 3. The anchors

Same 1 to 5 scale on every axis. No adjectives like "strong" or "excellent." Every level is
something a reviewer could point at in a week of work, which is what makes two different
reviewers land in the same place.

### Workstream Ownership

1. Waits to be told. Work stops when the next step is unclear. Blockers surface at the
   deadline rather than before it.
2. Does assigned tasks well but does not extend past them. Asks what to do next rather than
   proposing it.
3. Owns a defined piece and finishes it. Raises blockers early. Still needs the plan handed to
   them.
4. Owns a workstream end to end. Makes the plan, unblocks themselves, and reports what they
   decided rather than asking what to do.
5. Owns a workstream and pulls others along. Reprioritizes when the client changes direction,
   and what comes back is better than what was asked for.

### Collaboration

1. Works alone and the team finds out late. Takes feedback as criticism, or agrees with it and
   changes nothing.
2. Does their own part. Does not look at anyone else's. Applies feedback only where told to.
3. Reliable teammate. Reviews others' work when asked, shares context without being chased, and
   acts on feedback the next time.
4. Makes the people around them better. Asks for feedback before it is offered, and the
   correction is visible in the next piece of work.
5. Raises the standard of the whole team. Handles disagreement without it becoming personal,
   and changes position publicly when someone else is right.

### Communication

1. Updates are late or absent. The reader has to ask what happened.
2. Reports activity in the order it happened. The reader has to extract the point themselves.
3. Answer first, support underneath. Someone who reads only the first line knows the state of
   the work.
4. Writes for the specific reader. Names the decision being asked for, and the memo or deck
   holds up without anyone narrating it.
5. Produces the artifact the team reuses. A senior stakeholder can act on it without a meeting.

### Client Presence

1. Avoids client contact. Silent in the room, or speaks and creates confusion someone else
   repairs afterward.
2. Attends client meetings and answers direct questions. Arrives unprepared. Defers everything
   to the engagement lead.
3. Prepared and credible on their own work. Answers questions about what they built. Does not
   yet steer the conversation.
4. Runs a portion of the meeting. Sets expectations, says plainly what will not fit in the time
   available, and follows up when they said they would.
5. The client asks for this person by name. Surfaces a risk before the client finds it, and the
   relationship is stronger after the bad news than before it.

*"Client hands" is the industry phrase for the fourth axis. Worth teaching, not worth putting
on the chart.*

## 4. How feedback is gathered

Everyone who saw your work has a say, and the weight of their say depends on how much of your
work they saw.

### The running mechanism

Every two weeks, fifteen minutes of mutual feedback with the people on your pod, per the
constitution. This is not collected and it is not graded. It exists so that nothing in the
formal rounds is new information.

### The exposure list

Near the end of the semester, every student submits a list of everyone who saw their work:

| Source | Who | What they can speak to |
|---|---|---|
| **Peers** | Every Foundry member you worked with: pod teammates, engagement lead, QC lead, design lead | All four axes |
| **Clients** | Every client contact you worked with directly | Client Presence and Communication. The other two only if they genuinely saw them |
| **Faculty and TAs** | Scott, and any TA who supervised the pod | All four axes |
| **Self** | You | Your own four ratings, against the published anchors |

For each person on the list you record the project, the nature of the work, and the **exposure
depth**, which the professor confirms against staffing records:

- **Deep.** Worked together on the same engagement, most weeks, for most of the semester.
- **Moderate.** Same engagement for part of the term, or a different engagement with regular
  contact.
- **Light.** A handful of interactions. A review, a few meetings, one shared sprint.

You do not choose who reviews you. You report who saw the work, and the professor may add
anyone the staffing records show you worked with.

### The input itself

Deep-exposure reviewers are interviewed. Everyone else answers a written form built from the
same questions. Section 5 is the guide.

Reviewers are not shown the axes and are not asked to rate anyone. They are asked what the
person did. **The professor assigns the four ratings** against the published anchors, from the
evidence the conversation produced. An axis a reviewer gave no evidence on is left blank rather
than guessed.

Input goes to the professor only. Individual comments are never attributed back.

## 5. The interview guide

This is the professor's guide, not a student handout. It is what gets asked of the people on
the exposure list.

The principles are not read aloud and the axes are not announced. Reviewers are asked for
specific things a person did, and the principles surface in the answers on their own. A
reviewer who is told the framework starts producing evidence to fit it.

### How the input is collected

| Exposure | Method | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Deep | Live conversation, this guide | 20 minutes |
| Moderate | Live if schedulable, otherwise the written form | 10 minutes |
| Light | Written form, questions 1, 4, 8, 9 only | 5 minutes |
| Client | Live call, client version below | 15 minutes |

Ratings are entered by the professor after the conversation ends, not by the reviewer during
it. Get the story first. A reviewer who rates first then spends the rest of the conversation
defending the number.

**One interview covers everyone that reviewer worked with**, which is what makes this
affordable. A cohort of thirty on six pods is roughly thirty peer conversations plus six client
calls, not thirty times four. Call it twelve hours across the finals period, and the midterm
practice round is lighter because it only needs the deep sources.

### Opening

> This is confidential. Nothing you say gets attributed to you, and what comes back to them is
> a summary of themes across everyone who worked with them. I am going to ask about specific
> things that happened, not for an overall impression. If you cannot remember an instance, say
> so, that is a useful answer too.

The last sentence matters. It gives permission for "I never saw that," which is what keeps a
reviewer from inventing a middling answer to be polite.

### Workstream Ownership

1. **What did they own?** Walk me through a piece of work that was theirs end to end. Who
   decided what the steps were?
2. **Tell me about a time the work got stuck.** How did you find out? What did they do next?
3. **When the client changed direction or a deadline moved,** what did they do without being
   asked?

*Listening for: whether the plan came from them or was handed to them. Whether blockers arrived
early or at the deadline. Whether "not my job" ever appeared in any form.*

### Collaboration

4. **Would you staff them on your next project?** Why, specifically?
5. **Tell me about a disagreement on the team.** What was their part in it, and how did it end?
6. **When was the last time they changed their mind because of something a teammate said?**
   What about the reverse: what happened when someone gave them a correction?

*Listening for: whether the disagreement stayed about the work. Whether feedback produced a
visible change in the next piece of work or just a pleasant nod. Whether they made anyone else
better.*

### Communication

7. **Think of something they wrote, an update, a memo, a deck.** Could you tell the state of
   the work from the first few lines, or did you have to dig? Did you need them in the room for
   it to make sense?
8. **When something was going badly, how did you hear about it?** From them, from someone else,
   or from the result?

*Listening for: answer first versus activity log. Whether bad news traveled at the same speed
as good news.*

### Client Presence

9. **Describe them in front of the client.** What did they actually say and do?
10. **Was there a moment the client needed to hear something they would not like?** Who said
    it, and what happened after?
11. **Did they ever commit to something in a client meeting?** Did it happen when they said it
    would?

*Listening for: prepared or improvising. Whether the client's interest came ahead of looking
good. Whether a hard truth was delivered in a way the relationship survived.*

### Closing

12. **What is the one thing they should work on next semester?**
13. **Is there anything I did not ask about that I should know?**
14. **Who else saw their work closely?**

Question 12 is the most valuable one in the guide, because it is the sentence that goes into
the development summary almost verbatim. Question 14 validates the exposure list against
reality, and reviewers named by three different people who appear on nobody's list are worth
noticing.

### The client version

Clients rate Client Presence and Communication, and only rate the other two if they genuinely
saw them. Fifteen minutes, and it doubles as a relationship call.

1. How did they show up in meetings with you? Prepared, or figuring it out live?
2. When you needed something, did you go to them or around them?
3. Did anything they told you turn out to be more optimistic than reality?
4. Was there a moment they told you something you did not want to hear?
5. Would you want them on the next engagement?
6. What is one thing that would have made working with them better?

Question 3 is the one that gets the honest answer, because it asks about a thing that happened
rather than asking the client to criticize a student.

### Rules for the professor

- **Ask for an instance, not a trait.** "Tell me about a time" produces evidence. "Are they a
  good communicator" produces an adjective.
- **Follow the story, not the script.** These are the questions that must get answered, not the
  order they must be answered in.
- **Push once on a generality, then let it go.** "Can you think of a specific time?" A second
  push produces a manufactured example, which is worse than no example.
- **Do not defend the student.** The reviewer stops talking.
- **Write the rating within the hour.** Not during, not the next day.

## 6. How that becomes a grade

Three steps: a composite, a band, and a calibration.

### Step 1. The composite

For each axis, ratings are averaged with reviewers weighted by exposure depth:

| Exposure | Weight |
|---|---|
| Deep | 3 |
| Moderate | 2 |
| Light | 1 |

Clients are weighted Deep on the two axes they are qualified to rate. Your self-assessment is
**not** in the composite. It is compared against it, and a persistent gap in either direction
is a finding worth a conversation, not a penalty.

The composite is the mean of the four axis scores, weighted equally. No axis is worth more than
another, because a student who drives work nobody wants to receive and a student who is
delightful and ships nothing are two versions of the same failure.

**Coverage floor.** An axis with fewer than three independent reviewers is not scored from
input alone. The professor rates it directly and says so in the development summary.

### Step 2. The band

| Composite | Grade | Points |
|---|---|---|
| 4.5 to 5.0 | A | 4.0 |
| 4.1 to 4.4 | A- | 3.7 |
| 3.6 to 4.0 | B+ | 3.4 |
| 3.1 to 3.5 | B | 3.0 |
| 2.6 to 3.0 | B- | 2.7 |
| Below 2.6 | Reviewed individually | |

Read the anchors alongside the bands and the standard is plain. **A 3 is a reliable teammate
who does what is asked.** In a graduate class that is a B. **A 4 is someone who owns a
workstream, makes their team better, writes something a stakeholder can act on, and can be put
in front of a client.** That is the expectation, and it earns an A-. An A means close to a 4.5
across all four, which is rare on purpose.

These bands do not move. They are published in week 1 so every student knows exactly what an A
requires before they do any work.

**Two hard rules that override the band:**

- A **1 on any axis** from multiple independent reviewers caps the grade at B-, whatever the
  other three say. The axes are not tradeable at the bottom.
- Anything that would fail the red face test, or a confidentiality breach on client work, is a
  conduct matter and leaves this model entirely.

### Step 3. Calibration, and why there is no curve

**The bands above are absolute, published before the semester starts, and not moved after
grades are computed.** If every student in the section earns a 4.5 composite, every student
gets an A. Excellence over everything is the first principle in our constitution, and a model
that grades your excellence against your classmates' contradicts it. You are competing against
the anchors, not against each other.

That is also the only version of this that survives contact with a self-organizing team. The
moment a grade depends on the distribution, helping the person next to you costs you something,
and Collaboration becomes the axis the instrument itself punishes you for.

**Expected section GPA: 3.4 to 3.6 for the graduate section.**

That range is a **check on the instrument, not a quota on the students.** The anchors are
written so that a section doing good work lands inside it. After grades are computed the mean
is checked, and the result is read as information about the model:

| Section mean | What it means | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 3.4 to 3.6 | The anchors are calibrated | Nothing |
| Above 3.6 | Either an unusually strong cohort, or reviewers are inflating | Grades stand. Inspect which sources ran high, and tighten anchor wording for the next cohort |
| Below 3.4 | Either a struggling cohort, or the anchors ask too much | Grades stand. Same inspection, opposite direction |

Grades stand in both directions. A cohort is not penalized for a miscalibrated instrument, and
it is not clawed back for exceeding it.

Roughly, a section landing in that range looks like four in ten students earning some form of
A. That is what the anchors are tuned to produce. It is a prediction, not a target, and no
student's grade is ever set by reference to it.

**Inflation is the real risk, and it is managed by design, not by a curve.** Every rating
requires a specific example. Reviewers are weighted by exposure. Peer ratings sit alongside
independent client and faculty ratings, and where those diverge the divergence is visible.
Anchor 5 on every axis describes something rare and concrete, which is what keeps a friendly
teammate from handing out 5s without noticing they are claiming the client asks for this person
by name.

### What this is not

- **Not arithmetic.** The composite produces the band. The professor may move a student one
  step, in either direction, where the written evidence contradicts the numbers, and writes the
  reason in the development summary. More than one step means the input was wrong and the
  instrument needs fixing, not the student.
- **Not the whole course grade.** Milestone and client evaluation components grade the product
  and the team. This grades the person. See open question 1 on the split.
- **Not a peer popularity vote.** Reviewers rate against published anchors with examples,
  weighted by exposure, triangulated across peers, clients, and faculty. A student cannot be
  sunk by one teammate or floated by one friend.

### Development summary

Each student receives a written summary: their four axis scores, the themes across sources,
where sources disagreed, and the two things to work on next. Individual comments stay
confidential and unattributed.

**No surprises.** Because we live the Ask First culture and run mutual feedback every two weeks,
nothing in a final summary should be news. If it surprises you, you were not asking.

## 7. Timeline

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | The model, the four axes, and the anchors are explained to the full team |
| Midterm | Practice round. Draft exposure list and a light input pass, so everyone gets a snapshot while there is still time to act on it |
| Two weeks before the end of the semester | Final exposure list and self-assessment due |
| Finals period | Input gathered, grades determined, development summaries delivered |

The midterm round is the part that makes this developmental rather than judgmental. A rating
delivered only at the end is a verdict. A rating delivered at the midpoint is a chance.

## Open questions

1. **Weight.** What share of the course grade this carries against the milestone and client
   evaluation components. The syllabus currently says the Consultant ratings feed the existing
   Client Evaluation and Peer / Team Evaluation components without adding points. That is too
   quiet now that this is the whole individual instrument, and it has to be resolved before the
   500-point table on the syllabus is final.
2. **Whether students see their axis scores** or only the written development summary.
3. **Principle coverage.** If reviewers are never prompted for a specific principle, the loud
   ones get cited constantly and the quiet ones never do. Worth deciding whether one prompt in
   the input form asks for a principle by name.
4. **The undergraduate section.** The 3.4 to 3.6 range is the graduate expectation. STRAT 490R students
   sit in the same room, on the same pods, rated by the same reviewers against the same anchors.
   Whether their bands shift, and how that is explained when two students on one pod compare
   grades, is unresolved.
5. **MBA internal leadership.** MBAs are rated on the same four axes twice, once for client
   work and once for running the Foundry. Whether those combine into one composite or stay
   separate on the summary is unresolved.
6. **Discovery-only engagements.** A team whose client stops after discovery has less client
   contact to be rated on. Client Presence may need a coverage floor.
