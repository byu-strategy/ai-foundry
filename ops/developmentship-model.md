# The Developmentship Model

*Draft. Supersedes the Google Doc of the same name, 2026-08-12.*

Developmentship is our practice of developing one another through shared work, and it is how
your individual grade is determined.

Your grade is not the professor's opinion of you. It is a synthesis of structured input from
the people you actually worked with, gathered against four named things, every one of which is
something a person visibly does.

## 1. One instrument: the Consultant diamond

The model has one measuring instrument. Four axes, rated 1 to 5, observed by other people.

| Axis | Imperative behind it | Judged on |
|---|---|---|
| **Judgment** | Think Clearly, and Get to the Right Answer | Whether you solve the right problem and say what the facts support |
| **Ownership** | Move Work Forward | Whether you drive the work |
| **Communication** | Create Impact with People: do people *understand* it | The artifacts: what you produce for a reader (updates, decks, memos) |
| **Collaboration** | Create Impact with People: do people *trust* you | How your team and your client experience working with you, and whether they want you on the next one |

Four ratings, not sixteen. A reviewer can hold four judgments and mean them. Ask for sixteen
and they will straight-line the form.

The axes come from the Four Imperatives taught in STRAT 325. Two are about the work: Judgment
asks whether it is the right work, and Ownership asks whether it moves. Two are about people,
and they follow 325's guiding question for Create Impact with People, "Do people understand,
trust, and act on this work?" Communication is the *understand* half and is judged on the
artifact. Collaboration is the *trust* half and is judged on the relationship, with anyone you
work with, teammate or client.

An earlier draft had three people axes: Communication, Collaboration (teammates only), and
Client Hands (the client relationship). They were cut on two different lines, one by medium and
two by audience, so they overlapped, and the draft drew on only two of the four imperatives.
Judgment was missing. The client relationship now sits inside Collaboration, and clients rate
all four axes.

### Ten dimensions

Together with the six Builder axes (Discovery, Design, Application Architecture, AI Systems,
Agentic Workflow, Launch and Learn), that makes ten dimensions. The Builder axes are what you can
make, graded on artifacts in the Build course. The Consultant axes are how you work, observed by
the people around you in the Foundry. Judgment runs through every Builder axis, Discovery most of
all. That overlap is deliberate: the Build course grades judgment as it shows up in an artifact,
and this diamond rates it as it shows up in decisions other people watched you make.

### Why not the Builder hexagon

Not because building does not matter. Because the hexagon is graded in the other class, from
different evidence.

The rule across both courses is that **demonstrated proficiency is graded and a self-rating
never is.** In the Build course the six Builder axes organize the graded work: the sprints, the
codebase architecture analysis, peer code review, peer product testing. A student's standing on
an axis there comes from an artifact somebody else evaluated.

The baseline survey is a third thing, and it is graded nowhere. It is a development instrument
you use on yourself and an input to how you get staffed. The moment a self-report carries grade
weight it stops measuring anything, and grading the August-to-December change is worse, because
it rewards understating the baseline. It would also cost you the staffing signal, which is what
the survey is actually load-bearing for.

| Instrument | Graded on | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Builder hexagon | The artifacts you produced | Build course |
| Consultant diamond | The conduct others observed | The Foundry Lab |
| Baseline self-assessment | Not graded, anywhere | Survey, September and December |

Same six axes in the first and third rows, read two different ways. Keeping them apart is what
makes each one honest.

### Why not the deliverable

The client work is graded already, through the milestone and client evaluation components of
the syllabus. Those are team-level and they measure the product. This model is individual and
it measures the person. Do not make either one carry the other's job.

## 2. Where the principles live

The constitution is the standard. The four axes are where it becomes visible.

**Nobody is rated on a principle, because a principle is not a thing you can rate.** It is a
thing you can point at. So there is no fifth axis and there is no values score. A student living
the principles rates high on this diamond. A student rating low is failing a principle that can
be named. That coupling is the whole design, and it is why the model is worth running.

### The principle citation

The principles do appear on all three forms, once, and never as a rating. Every reviewer is
asked the same optional question: **which principle did you see this person live, and what did
they do?** A dropdown of the ten, plus a required instance if one is selected.

**It is positive only.** No form in this system asks which principle someone failed. That
constraint is doing real work:

- **It cannot become a character judgment.** A student is never recorded anywhere as having
  failed a value by peer vote. Rating each other's adherence to values, at this university, with
  grade weight attached, is one inference away from rating each other's worthiness. We are not
  going to build that instrument.
- **It stays out of the grade honestly.** Because there is no negative side, the citations
  cannot be summed into anything. Absence of a citation means a reviewer had nothing to hand,
  which is not evidence of a failure.
- **Principle failures are binary, and they escalate.** Hiding a mistake, overselling to a
  client, a confidentiality breach. These are conduct, they are raised directly, and averaging
  them into a composite would launder them. They never travel through a survey.

Clients get the same question in plain language, because they have never read our constitution
and are never shown it: *did anyone do something that stuck with you?* We do the tagging.

### What the citations are for

Coverage, not score. Across a cohort they answer a question no rating can: **which of our
principles are actually visible in the work, and which ones do we only talk about.**

| Read | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Per student | The language of their development summary, in their reviewers' own words |
| Per student, against their self-citation | A principle they claim that nobody else saw. A conversation, never a penalty |
| Per team | Whether a team lived a principle or merely had it on the wall |
| Per cohort, per principle | Which principles the Foundry is actually teaching. A principle nobody cites all year is either not visible in the work or not real |

The last row is the reason this is worth collecting. If Candor with charity gets cited four
times in a year and Own your outcomes gets cited eighty, that is information about the studio,
not about the students.

**Citations are not scored and no count moves a composite.** They are written evidence, and the
professor already has the discretion in section 6 to move a student one band where the written
evidence contradicts the numbers. That is the only path by which a citation touches a grade, and
it is the same path every other comment on these forms travels.

## 3. The anchors

Same 1 to 5 scale on every axis. No adjectives like "strong" or "excellent." Every level is
something a reviewer could point at in a week of work, which is what makes two different
reviewers land in the same place.

### Judgment

1. Solves the problem as handed over, even when it is the wrong one. Conclusions run ahead of
   the evidence.
2. Sound on the task in front of them, but does not ask whether it is the right task. Reports
   what the data says, not what it means.
3. Checks that the question is the right one before starting. Conclusions follow from the
   evidence, and they say where it is thin.
4. Reframes the problem when the facts call for it. Separates what is known from what is
   assumed; recommendations hold up when tested.
5. The team or client changes direction because of how they framed it. Says what the facts
   support when it is not what anyone hoped.

### Ownership

1. Waits to be told. Work stops when the next step is unclear. Blockers surface at the
   deadline rather than before it.
2. Does assigned tasks well but does not extend past them. Asks what to do next rather than
   proposing it.
3. Owns a defined piece and finishes it. Raises blockers early. Still needs the plan handed to
   them.
4. Owns a workstream end to end. Makes the plan, unblocks themselves, and reports what they
   decided rather than asking what to do.
5. Owns a workstream and pulls others along. Reprioritizes when the client changes direction,
   and what comes back is better than what was asked for.

### Communication

1. Updates are late or absent. The reader has to ask what happened.
2. Reports activity in the order it happened. The reader has to extract the point themselves.
3. Answer first, support underneath. Someone who reads only the first line knows the state of
   the work.
4. Writes for the specific reader. Names the decision being asked for, and the memo or deck
   holds up without anyone narrating it.
5. Produces the artifact the team reuses. A senior stakeholder can act on it without a meeting.

### Collaboration

1. Works alone; the team and the client find out late. Takes feedback as criticism, or agrees
   and changes nothing.
2. Does their own part and reacts when asked. Leaves teammates' work and the client
   relationship to others; applies feedback only where told.
3. Reliable to work with. Keeps commitments to teammates and the client, shares context without
   being chased, and acts on feedback the next time.
4. Makes the people around them better. Asks for feedback before it is offered, says early what
   will not fit, and teammates and the client come to them directly.
5. People ask to work with them again, by name. Handles disagreement without it becoming
   personal, changes position when someone else is right, and surfaces a risk before anyone else
   finds it.

Collaboration covers everyone you work with, teammates and the client alike, and it is rated by
both. It is about the trust built over the whole engagement, so a student can be quiet in a
client meeting and still rate a 4 if the client relies on them between meetings.

## 4. How input is gathered

Everyone who saw your work has a say, and the weight of their say depends on how much of your
work they saw.

### The running mechanism

Every two weeks, fifteen minutes of mutual feedback with your teammate, per the
constitution. This is not collected and it is not graded. It exists so that nothing in the
formal rounds is new information.

### The exposure list

Every student keeps an exposure list in the Cockpit all semester: everyone who saw their work,
added as the work happens rather than reconstructed at the end.

| Source | Who | Can speak to |
|---|---|---|
| **Peers** | Every Foundry member you worked with: your teammate on each engagement, and anyone on an internal project or who reviewed your work | All four axes |
| **Clients** | Every client contact you worked with directly | All four axes |
| **Faculty** | Scott, when he chooses to. Optional, never required | All four axes |
| **Self** | You | Your own four ratings, against the published anchors |

For each person you record whether they are a peer or a client, the **context** (which client
engagement or internal project), how they saw the work, and the **exposure depth**:

- **Deep.** Same engagement or project, most of its weeks.
- **Moderate.** Part of an engagement, or regular contact from a different one.
- **Light.** A handful of interactions. A review, a few meetings, one shared sprint.

The Cockpit suggests names from staffing records. The professor approves each new name and adds
anyone staffing shows you worked with. You do not choose who reviews you. You report who saw
the work, and leaving someone off does not remove them.

### When the survey goes out

Each person on the list gets the survey **when your work together ends**: at roll-off for client
work, or when you mark an internal project done. Peers see it on their Cockpit page under
"Feedback you owe" and by email. Clients get one email with a private link, no sign-in, and one
form covers every student on the team who listed them. Reminders go at three and seven days.

Someone who worked with you in two contexts rates each one, but counts as **one person** toward
the thresholds below.

### Two releases a semester

Responses arrive all semester. You see them twice: at the **midterm release** and the **final
release**. Between releases nothing you can see changes, so the arrival of any one response is
invisible.

- **You see the combined result only,** as the Consultant diamond, with the number of people
  behind each axis. Never who said what, never the split by source.
- **An axis appears once three different people have rated it.** Until then it reads "not
  enough ratings yet."
- **An axis changes at the final release only if at least two more people have rated it since
  midterm.** If one person had rated you in between, the change would be their rating, exactly.
  Held back, it still counts toward your grade; it just does not move the chart.
- **Written comments go to the professor only,** and come back to you as themes in your
  development summary, never word for word.
- **The professor sees everything as it arrives,** attributed, by source.

Teams are two people and engagements are six weeks, so one engagement alone rarely produces
three raters. That is why the list and the thresholds run across the whole semester.

### Three instruments, one question set

Three sources rate you, each through its own form, and every form is built from the same
underlying questions. That is what makes this one system rather than three surveys that happen
to be scheduled together.

| Instrument | Who fills it | Rates | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Self-Assessment](self-assessment-survey.md) | You | All four axes. **Not in your composite** | ~20 min, once |
| [Peer Evaluation](peer-evaluation-survey.md) | Every Foundry member on your exposure list | All four axes | ~5 min per person, when the shared work ends |
| [Client Feedback](client-feedback.md) | Every client contact on an exposure list | All four axes, through plain-language items (Ownership from Timeliness and High Agency, Collaboration from Working with you and Taking feedback) | ~5 to 8 min, at the end of every 5-week block |

All three are built into the Cockpit, not Google Forms: the exposure list, the thresholds, and
the release rules all run on its data.

**Order matters.** Your self-assessment closes before each release, so it is always produced
before you have seen anything. Your self-rating is worthless as a measure of calibration if it
was produced after you read your reviews.

### The same question, three ways

For each axis there is one underlying behavioral question, asked from the vantage each rater
actually has. This is the coherence spine of the model.

| Axis | Peers are asked | The client is asked | You are asked |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Judgment** | Did they solve the right problem, and say what the facts supported? | Did their thinking hold up? Did they tell you what the facts supported, even when it was not what you hoped? | Point to a decision where your framing or your reading of the facts changed what the team or client did |
| **Ownership** | Did they drive the work, or wait to be told? | Did they do what they said, when they said? Did they move the work forward on their own? | What did you own end to end that nobody had to chase you about? |
| **Communication** | Could you tell the state of the work from what they wrote? | Could you act on what they sent you without them in the room? | Point to one thing you wrote that someone acted on |
| **Collaboration** | Did they make the people around them better, and do you want them on your next team? | How was this person to work with, and did you come to trust them? When you pushed back, how did they respond? | How did you earn the trust of your team and your client, and how could you tell? |

Peers and clients are asked what they **observed**. You are asked to **produce evidence**. That
asymmetry is deliberate: producing an instance is a harder task than recognizing one, and it is
the only form of self-rating that resists wishful thinking.

The client form alone hides the framework. Clients never see an axis name or an anchor, because
a busy executive applying our rubric is a worse measurement than a busy executive telling us
what happened.

### Forms first, interviews where they change something

Reviewers rate directly against the published anchors. Peers and faculty are shown the anchors
because they were taught them in week 1, and an anchor is only an anti-inflation device if the
rater can see what a 5 actually claims.

**The professor interviews in four cases, not thirty:**

1. **Every client, once per round.** Fifteen minutes, and it doubles as a renewal and referral
   call. This one is not about grades and would be worth doing if grades did not exist.
2. **Material disagreement between sources.** The team rates someone 4.5 and the client says they
   would not have them back, or the reverse. A large calibration gap counts here too.
3. **A composite within 0.25 of a cap boundary.** Never let a form alone decide the difference
   between a B and a B-.
4. **On request.** Any student may ask for one, and any reviewer may ask to speak rather than
   type.

Everything else runs on the forms. A cohort of thirty produces roughly six client calls and a
handful of escalations per round, not thirty conversations.

**Ratings that arrive without required evidence are dropped from the composite,** not averaged
in. A 5 with no instance behind it is an opinion, and the whole design is built to exclude
opinions.

Input goes to the professor only. Individual comments are never attributed back.

## 5. The interview guide

This is the professor's guide for the cases in section 4 where an interview runs. It is not a
student handout and it is not the default path.

The principles are not read aloud and the axes are not announced. Reviewers are asked for
specific things a person did, and the principles surface in the answers on their own. A
reviewer who is told the framework starts producing evidence to fit it.

### Opening

> This is confidential. Nothing you say gets attributed to you, and what comes back to them is a
> summary of themes across everyone who worked with them. I am going to ask about specific
> things that happened, not for an overall impression. If you cannot remember an instance, say
> so, that is a useful answer too.

The last sentence matters. It gives permission for "I never saw that," which is what keeps a
reviewer from inventing a middling answer to be polite.

### Judgment

1. **Was there a point where they asked whether the team was solving the right problem?** What
   prompted it, and what happened next?
2. **Think of a recommendation they made, or a conclusion they drew from data.** Did it hold up?
   Did they say where the evidence was thin? Was there something the client needed to hear that
   they would not like, and who said it?

*Listening for: whether the framing came from them or was handed to them. Whether conclusions
stayed inside the evidence. Whether an unwelcome finding was stated plainly or softened away.*

### Ownership

3. **What did they own?** Walk me through a piece of work that was theirs end to end. Who
   decided what the steps were?
4. **Tell me about a time the work got stuck.** How did you find out? What did they do next?
5. **When the client changed direction or a deadline moved,** what did they do without being
   asked? If they committed to something in a client meeting, did it happen when they said?

*Listening for: whether the plan came from them or was handed to them. Whether blockers arrived
early or at the deadline. Whether "not my job" ever appeared in any form.*

### Communication

6. **Think of something they wrote, an update, a memo, a deck.** Could you tell the state of the
   work from the first few lines, or did you have to dig? Did you need them in the room for it to
   make sense?
7. **When something was going badly, how did you hear about it?** From them, from someone else,
   or from the result?

*Listening for: answer first versus activity log. Whether bad news traveled at the same speed as
good news.*

### Collaboration

8. **Would you staff them on your next project?** Why, specifically?
9. **How did the client's trust in them change over the engagement?** What did they do that
   earned it, or cost it? Did the client start coming to them directly?
10. **Tell me about a disagreement, on the team or with the client.** What was their part in it,
    and how did it end?
11. **When was the last time they changed their mind because of something a teammate or the
    client said?** What happened when someone gave them a correction?

*Listening for: whether the disagreement stayed about the work. Whether feedback produced a
visible change in the next piece of work or just a pleasant nod. Whether the client's interest
came ahead of looking good.*

### Closing

12. **What is the one thing they should work on next semester?**
13. **Is there anything I did not ask about that I should know?**
14. **Who else saw their work closely?**

Question 12 is the most valuable one in the guide, because it is the sentence that goes into the
development summary almost verbatim. It is also question 3 of the peer form, which is why the
form can substitute for the interview in most cases. Question 14 validates the exposure list
against reality.

### The client call

Runs against [Client Feedback](client-feedback.md) rather than this guide. Its event questions
are the interview: what the team was optimistic about, who the client went to when they needed
something, and what hard thing got said. Fifteen minutes, and it doubles as
a relationship call.

### Rules for the professor

- **Ask for an instance, not a trait.** "Tell me about a time" produces evidence. "Are they a
  good communicator" produces an adjective.
- **Follow the story, not the script.** These are the questions that must get answered, not the
  order they must be answered in.
- **Push once on a generality, then let it go.** A second push produces a manufactured example,
  which is worse than no example.
- **Do not defend the student.** The reviewer stops talking.
- **Write the rating within the hour.** Not during, not the next day.

## 6. How that becomes a grade

Three steps: a composite, a conversion to points, and a set of caps that the points cannot
override.

### Step 1. The composite

You will have several peer reviewers, sometimes several client contacts, and one or two faculty.
They are not thrown into one average, because several peers and one client would let the peers
outvote the person paying for the work. Aggregation runs in three passes.

#### Pass 1. Aggregate inside each source

Within a source, ratings are averaged with reviewers weighted by exposure depth:

| Exposure | Weight |
|---|---|
| Deep. Same engagement, most weeks, most of the semester | 3 |
| Moderate. Part of the term, or regular contact on another engagement | 2 |
| Light. A handful of interactions | 1 |

Client contacts are all weighted Deep on the axes they are qualified to rate, because a client
who worked with you at all worked with you in the situation the axis is about. Multiple client
contacts on one engagement average together into a single client score. Multiple engagements
average together, weighted by how long you were on each.

This pass produces at most four numbers per axis: **a peer score, a client score, a faculty
score, and your self score.**

#### Pass 2. Combine the sources, per axis

Sources are weighted by what they can actually see. Peers see the most of your work, so they
carry the most weight on every axis, and the most on Ownership, which a client sees least. The
client is close behind on Communication and Collaboration, where they are the reader and the
other half of the relationship. Faculty ratings are optional: when Scott rates, his rating is one
more source at the weight shown, and when he does not, the other weights scale up.

| Axis | Peers | Client | Faculty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Judgment | 40% | 30% | 30% |
| Ownership | 50% | 20% | 30% |
| Communication | 40% | 35% | 25% |
| Collaboration | 40% | 35% | 25% |

**Missing sources are reweighted, not zeroed.** If a client skipped the Ownership question, or
your engagement stopped after discovery and there is no meaningful client contact, the remaining
weights scale up proportionally. A student is never penalized for a source that did not exist.
On a team of one there is no peer on the engagement, so the client carries every axis for that
engagement, with faculty if Scott rated. That is intended.

Your self score is **not** in this calculation. It sits beside the result.

#### Pass 3. Average the axes

The composite is the mean of the four axis scores, weighted equally. No axis is worth more than
another, because a student who drives work nobody wants to receive and a student who is
delightful and ships nothing are two versions of the same failure. If an axis is held by the coverage
floor, the composite is the mean of the axes that are scored.

#### The guardrails on the aggregation

**Coverage floor.** An axis with fewer than three independent reviewers is held: it shows as
"not enough raters yet" and is left out of the composite, which then averages the axes that are
scored. Faculty may add a rating to reach the floor but never has to. The client side of
Collaboration on a discovery-only engagement is the case this exists for.

**Unsupported extremes are dropped.** A 5, a 1, or a 2 submitted without the required specific
instance does not enter any average.

**Divergence is a finding, not an error.** Where the peer score and the client score on the same
axis differ by more than 1.0, the professor interviews before the composite is finalized. The
common pattern is a student the team loves and the client never hears from, and the instrument is
built to make that visible rather than average it away.

### The calibration gap

Your self score is compared against your composite and reported to you as a gap.

| Gap (self minus actual) | Reading |
|---|---|
| +1.0 or more | Overrating. Named directly in the development summary and in the midterm conversation |
| +0.3 to +0.9 | Normal. Almost everyone rates themselves slightly high |
| -0.3 to +0.3 | Calibrated |
| -0.4 or lower | Underrating, which usually shows up as not volunteering for work you can do |

**The gap never changes your composite, in either direction.** What it changes is what your
development summary says and what your video reflection has to account for. Self-awareness is
graded once, in the reflection, on how honestly you engage with the distance between what you
predicted and what came back.

### Step 2. The conversion

The composite converts to the **Developmentship** component of the course grade, worth 180 of
500 points. It does not produce a separate letter grade. There is one grade in this course and
this is the largest single input to it.

| Composite | Points, of 180 | Equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| 4.75 to 5.00 | 180 | 100% |
| 4.50 to 4.74 | 173 | 96% |
| 4.25 to 4.49 | 168 | 93% |
| 4.00 to 4.24 | 162 | 90% |
| 3.75 to 3.99 | 156 | 87% |
| 3.50 to 3.74 | 150 | 83% |
| 3.25 to 3.49 | 144 | 80% |
| 3.00 to 3.24 | 138 | 77% |
| 2.75 to 2.99 | 131 | 73% |
| 2.50 to 2.74 | 126 | 70% |
| 2.00 to 2.49 | 113 | 63% |
| Below 2.00 | Reviewed individually | |

Read the anchors alongside this table and the standard is plain. **A 3 is a reliable teammate
who does what is asked**, and it converts at 77%. **A 4 is someone who reframes the problem when
the facts call for it, owns a workstream, writes something a stakeholder can act on, and makes
the team and the client around them better.** That is the expectation, and it converts at 90%.
Above 4.5 is rare on purpose.

This table does not move. It is published in week 1 so every student knows what an A requires
before they do any work.

### Step 3. The caps

Points alone would let a strong team carry a weak individual to an A-. These override the
arithmetic.

| Condition | Effect on the course grade |
|---|---|
| Composite below 3.00 | Capped at B, whatever the team components say |
| A rating of 1 on any axis from two or more independent reviewers | Capped at B-. The axes are not tradeable at the bottom |
| Composite below 2.00 | Individual review. A failing grade is available |
| Confidentiality breach, or anything failing the red face test | Leaves this model entirely. Conduct matter |

The first cap is the one that makes the instrument real. Without it, a student can coast on a
team that ships well, and every principle in the constitution becomes advisory.

### Step 4. Calibration, and why there is no curve

**The conversion table is absolute, published before the semester starts, and not moved after
grades are computed.** If every student in the section earns a 4.5 composite, every student gets
an A. Excellence over everything is the first principle in our constitution, and a model that
grades your excellence against your classmates' contradicts it. You are competing against the
anchors, not against each other.

That is also the only version of this that survives contact with a self-organizing team. The
moment a grade depends on the distribution, helping the person next to you costs you something,
and Collaboration becomes the axis the instrument itself punishes you for.

**Expected section GPA: 3.4 to 3.6 for the graduate section.** That range is a **check on the
instrument, not a quota on the students.**

| Section mean | What it means | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 3.4 to 3.6 | The anchors are calibrated | Nothing |
| Above 3.6 | Either an unusually strong cohort, or reviewers are inflating | Grades stand. Inspect which sources ran high, tighten anchor wording for the next cohort |
| Below 3.4 | Either a struggling cohort, or the anchors ask too much | Grades stand. Same inspection, opposite direction |

Grades stand in both directions. A cohort is not penalized for a miscalibrated instrument, and
it is not clawed back for exceeding it.

**Inflation is managed by design, not by a curve.** Extreme ratings require a specific instance
or they are dropped. Reviewers are weighted by exposure. Peer ratings sit alongside independent
client and faculty ratings, and where those diverge the divergence is visible and triggers an
interview. The peer form's 100-point split is zero-sum between teammates, so a pair that rates
each other a 5 still has to say how the work was actually divided.

### What this is not

- **Not arithmetic.** The composite produces the points. The professor may move a student one
  band, in either direction, where the written evidence contradicts the numbers, and writes the
  reason in the development summary. More than one band means the input was wrong and the
  instrument needs fixing, not the student.
- **Not the whole course grade.** It is 36% of it. Milestones and the client's team score grade
  the product and the team. This grades the person.
- **Not a peer popularity vote.** Reviewers rate against published anchors with required
  examples, weighted by exposure, triangulated across peers, clients, and faculty. A student
  cannot be sunk by one teammate or floated by one friend.
- **Not connected to stipends.** Stipends come from client weeks with utilization credit.
  Nothing on the peer form moves anyone's money.

### Development summary

Each student receives a written summary: their four axis scores, their calibration gap, the
themes across sources, where sources disagreed, the principles their reviewers cited with the
instances behind them, and the two things to work on next. Individual comments stay confidential
and unattributed.

The principle block is the part students tend to keep. It is the only place anyone tells them,
in specific terms and in someone else's words, that a thing they did was the constitution
happening rather than just the work getting done.

**No surprises.** Because we live the Ask First culture and run mutual feedback every two weeks,
nothing in a final summary should be news. If it surprises you, you were not asking.

| Week 1 | The model, the four axes, the anchors, and the conversion table are explained to everyone. Exposure lists open |
| All semester | Add people to your list as you work with them. Surveys go out as each engagement or project ends |
| Before the midterm release | Self-Assessment closes |
| Midterm release | Your diamond as of midterm, every axis with three or more raters. No points |
| Before the final release | Self-Assessment closes again. The professor confirms every list against staffing records |
| Final release | The diamond updates where at least two more people have rated you |
| Finals period | Client calls, escalation interviews, composites computed from every response, development summaries delivered |
| One week after summaries | Video reflections due |

The midterm release is what makes this developmental rather than judgmental. A rating delivered
only at the end is a verdict. A rating delivered at the midpoint is a chance.

## Open questions

1. **The undergraduate section.** The 3.4 to 3.6 range is the graduate expectation. STRAT 490R
   students sit in the same room, on the same teams, rated by the same reviewers against the same
   anchors. Whether their conversion table shifts, and how that is explained when two students on
   one team compare grades, is unresolved.
2. **MBA internal leadership.** MBAs are rated on the same four axes twice, once for client work
   and once for running the Foundry. Whether those combine into one composite or stay separate on
   the summary is unresolved.
3. **Discovery-only engagements.** A team whose client stops after discovery has less client
   contact to be rated on. The coverage floor holds any axis with fewer than three reviewers, so
   those students may finish with a composite built from fewer axes, and their summaries should
   say so.
4. **Release dates.** Midterm and final release dates are not yet set on the calendar.
5. **Whether the coverage data is published back to the cohort.** The per-principle counts
   across a whole cohort are the most interesting thing this system produces and say more about
   how the Foundry is being run than about any student. Showing them to everyone would be in the
   spirit of radical truthfulness. It would also tell students which principles get cited, which
   is exactly how you corrupt the measure. Unresolved.
6. **Whether the midterm release carries any points.** Currently it carries none, which is what
   makes it safe to be honest in. The risk is that a zero-stakes round gets filled out carelessly
   and the midterm diamond is worthless.
